*Why I aggressively short into strength instead of waiting for the reversal.*

A stock can be up 200%, climbing toward new highs, and attracting buyers from every direction.

Most traders see opportunity on the long side.

I see potential opportunity on the short side.

I'm Justin, The Bear of Wall Street. I specialize in short selling overextended small-cap stocks, and approximately **95% of my trades are taken on the frontside of the move.**

That's intentional.

While many short sellers wait for a stock to break down before entering, I prefer to anticipate areas of resistance, build positions into strength, and capitalize on the eventual fade.

It's an aggressive approach, but one I've built around planned entries, strategic scaling, and controlling losses.

Understanding the difference between frontside and backside short selling is fundamental to understanding how I trade.

## What is the frontside of a stock?

The frontside is the portion of a stock's move where upward momentum is still intact.

A company releases news, volume surges, and buyers begin chasing the stock higher.

Price makes higher highs, dips get bought, and momentum traders pile in.

A stock that opened at $2 might trade to $3, $4, or even $6 before the move finally exhausts itself.

For most traders, shorting during this phase sounds crazy.

**For me, it's where I spend almost all my time.**

I'm not waiting for every indicator to turn bearish. I'm looking for areas where the stock could encounter meaningful selling pressure while momentum traders are still aggressively buying.

The objective is to position myself around resistance before the broader reversal becomes obvious.

But there's an important distinction.

I'm not shorting a stock simply because it's up 100%.

I'm shorting at levels where I believe the potential reward outweighs the risk.

## Why I prefer aggressive frontside shorting

There's a reason I gravitate toward the frontside.

Some of the most explosive small-cap runners experience enormous intraday price swings. When momentum eventually fades, those reversals can be substantial.

By establishing positions around resistance while the stock is still moving higher, I can potentially capture a larger portion of that reversal.

Waiting for the backside may provide additional confirmation, but that confirmation comes at a cost.

By the time a stock loses VWAP, establishes lower highs, and clearly breaks its uptrend, a significant portion of the move may already be over.

My approach is different.

**I want to identify where buyers are likely to run into trouble before everyone else recognizes the reversal.**

That doesn't mean I'm always right.

Sometimes resistance breaks. Sometimes a stock runs significantly farther than expected. Sometimes the catalyst is strong enough to sustain momentum much longer than anticipated.

That's why frontside shorting demands preparation, experience, and disciplined risk management.

Aggression without a plan is just gambling.

## How I approach a frontside short

The foundation of my strategy is identifying meaningful resistance and building a trade around it.

Before entering, I'm evaluating several factors.

### 1. Important resistance levels

I pay close attention to areas where price may struggle.

These can include:

- **High-of-day levels:** Previous highs where momentum stalled or selling pressure appeared.
- **Daily chart resistance:** Significant historical price levels, including the 200-day moving average.
- **Whole-dollar levels:** Prices like $3, $4, and $5 that can attract substantial trading activity.
- **Previous failed breakouts:** Areas where buyers attempted to push higher but couldn't sustain the move.
- **Premarket highs:** Important reference points when a stock is moving aggressively before the opening bell.

These levels help me identify where a favorable short entry might develop.

But a resistance level isn't a guarantee.

The key is understanding how price behaves as it approaches that level.

### 2. Scaling into strength

This is one of the defining characteristics of my trading.

Rather than trying to perfectly time a short entry with my entire position, I often scale into a trade at predetermined levels.

Here's a hypothetical example.

A stock is trading at $2.50 and has significant resistance between $2.60 and $3.00.

Instead of immediately establishing a full position, I might plan entries at multiple prices within that range.

As the stock extends higher into resistance, I can add exposure at progressively higher prices, provided the trade remains within my predetermined risk limits.

This can improve my average entry while giving the stock room to fluctuate.

But scaling requires discipline.

If you continue adding simply because you're losing money, you can turn a manageable loss into a much larger one.

**There is a massive difference between planned scaling and desperately averaging into a losing position.**

Every addition should be part of the original trade plan.

And every trade needs an invalidation point.

### 3. Understanding the catalyst

Not every stock that runs is created equal.

I want to know why it's moving.

Is there a legitimate business development? A binding commercial agreement? Strong clinical data? A financing announcement? Or is the move largely speculative?

I also consider market capitalization, float, trading volume, share structure, and potential dilution.

None of these factors guarantees that a stock will fade.

But understanding the catalyst and the underlying company helps me evaluate whether the move has the potential to sustain itself.

The strongest catalysts deserve respect, even when the chart looks overextended.

### 4. Recognizing when the trade is wrong

This is where aggressive traders separate themselves from reckless ones.

I can be extremely aggressive with my execution while still being disciplined about when the trade no longer makes sense.

If a stock breaks through important resistance, sustains momentum, or invalidates my planned setup, I have to respond.

I don't get to decide that the market is wrong.

**You can be right about the eventual collapse and still lose money trying to short it too early.**

Recognizing that distinction is essential.

## What is backside short selling?

The backside begins when upward momentum starts deteriorating and sellers gain control.

Instead of consistently making higher highs, the stock begins establishing lower highs.

Previous support levels break. Bounces struggle. Buyers become less aggressive.

A typical backside setup might involve a stock losing VWAP, failing to reclaim it, and rejecting a bounce into previous support.

These are legitimate opportunities.

And for traders who prefer more confirmation, backside shorting can be a better fit.

But it's important to understand that **backside confirmation is not the foundation of my strategy.**

I primarily trade the frontside because that's where I've developed my approach and experience.

The backside is another tool, not my primary playbook.

## Frontside vs. backside: Which is better?

Neither approach is universally superior.

They offer different trade-offs.


| Frontside shorting | Backside shorting |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Anticipates resistance | Waits for signs of reversal |
| Entries while momentum remains strong | Entries after weakness develops |
| Potential to capture more of the fade | May miss the initial reversal |
| Greater exposure to continued squeezes | Greater evidence of seller control |
| Requires proactive risk management | Still requires clearly defined risk |


I prefer the frontside because it aligns with my strategy, my experience, and the setups I've spent years studying.

That doesn't make it automatically better for another trader.

A strategy only has value if you can execute it consistently while managing the associated risk.

## The biggest misconception about aggressive short selling

People sometimes assume aggressive trading simply means using larger positions or taking excessive risks.

That's not how I define aggression.

Aggression means recognizing an opportunity and executing decisively when the setup supports it.

It means being willing to short into strength when most traders are afraid to do so.

It means strategically scaling into planned resistance levels rather than waiting for the move to become obvious.

But aggression also means being decisive when the trade stops working.

**Aggression doesn't mean more size. It means conviction backed by preparation.**

A trader who refuses to exit a losing position isn't being aggressive.

They're being stubborn.

And the market has a way of making stubborn traders pay.

## What invalidates a frontside short?

Before entering a position, I want to know exactly what would make my original thesis invalid.

Depending on the setup, that could involve a sustained breakout through resistance, an unexpected strengthening of the catalyst, a change in price structure, or reaching my predefined risk limit.

I also consider the float and liquidity of the stock.

Extremely low-float stocks can experience sharp squeezes, rapid price movements, and trading halts.

Borrow costs, locate availability, and liquidity also factor into my decisions.

That's why position sizing and predefined risk are so important.

I'm willing to be aggressive.

I'm not willing to pretend I'm incapable of being wrong.

## Why you don't have to wait for the backside

There's an entire philosophy of trading built around waiting for confirmation.

And for many traders, that approach makes sense.

But it's not the only way to develop an edge.

I specialize in identifying areas where momentum may exhaust itself before the reversal is confirmed.

Sometimes I catch the move near its highs.

Sometimes I enter too early and have to adjust or exit.

Sometimes the setup fails completely.

That's part of trading.

The goal isn't to predict every top perfectly.

It's to develop a repeatable process around identifiable opportunities, manage risk when the thesis fails, and remain disciplined over a large sample of trades.

**You don't need to trade the way everyone else trades to develop an edge. You need to understand the edge you're actually trading.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Is frontside short selling riskier than backside shorting?

Frontside trading involves entering while upward momentum is still intact, so traders must account for continued price extensions. Backside trading provides additional confirmation of weakness but often comes with less favorable entry prices. Both approaches require a defined strategy and risk management.

### Why short a stock that's still going up?

A frontside short seller may identify resistance, overextension, or potential exhaustion before the broader market recognizes a reversal. The objective is to enter around a favorable level rather than wait until the reversal becomes obvious. Having an invalidation level helps manage the trade if the stock continues higher.

### Is scaling into a short position the same as averaging up?

Scaling into strength can involve averaging up, but the important distinction is whether additional entries were planned around specific levels and risk limits. Unplanned additions to a losing trade can dramatically increase exposure.

## The bottom line

Approximately 95% of my trades are frontside shorts.

I don't need a stock to establish a confirmed downtrend before I become interested.

I want to understand the catalyst, identify significant resistance, anticipate where momentum may struggle, and execute an aggressive but structured trading plan.

Sometimes that means entering while a stock is still making higher highs.

Sometimes it means scaling into strength.

And sometimes it means recognizing that I'm wrong and getting out.

**The edge isn't simply being aggressive. It's knowing when aggression is justified and when to walk away.**

That's the foundation of how I approach small-cap short selling.
